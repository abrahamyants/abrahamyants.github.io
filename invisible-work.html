THE STARTING POINT

A model that behaves
because you corrected it
741 times is not safe.

The gap

Anthropic's own evaluations, described in the Claude Opus 4.5 system card, run two open-source tools: Petri, where an auditor agent holds simulated conversations with a model and judges score the transcript, and Bloom, which generates many scenarios for one behavior and reports how often it shows up. Both measure one side of the exchange: what the model did. In January 2026 Anthropic's "Who's in Charge?" research named the blind spot in words: safeguards work at the single-exchange level and miss what builds up across turns.

Nobody was asking the other question. What did the human have to do to get that clean output? A model that produces good work after ten corrections and a model that produces good work after 741 score the same on every existing eval. They are not the same.

My part

I had something a lab does not: six months of my own real working conversations with two models, on code, compliance, writing, and research. I built Drift Auditor on the same model-evaluating-model idea as Bloom, added a second taxonomy for the human's moves, ran it on all 512 conversations, and wrote up what came out, limits included. The paper went to Anthropic's research team.

Ten tags for the model

Sycophancy, reality distortion, confidence inflation, instruction drop, semantic dilution, correction decay, conflict pair, shadow pattern, operator move, void. The first six extend categories that exist. The last four are new. One of them, the operator move, classifies what the human did instead of the model.

Twelve rules for the human

Anchor, echo check, boundary, correction, not-shot, contrastive, reset, decompose, evidence demand, meta call, tiger tamer, kill switch. Each one is a kind of steering a person does to keep a model on task. They came from my earlier field manual, 12 Rules for AI.

The numbers that did not exist

Operator Load Index: interventions per message. Alignment Tax: the share of a conversation spent steering. Instruction Survival Rate: how many instructions were still being followed. Self-Sufficiency Score: how much of the work the model carried on its own. Correction Efficiency: how many corrections held.

THE FINDING

Same drift score.
Different failure.

Claude 2.9 out of 10. ChatGPT 3.0.
Stop there and the models look equal. They are not.

Claude fails loudly

741 corrections. A sixth of every conversation spent steering. It needs one and a half times the intervention. But it keeps more of what you tell it, and when you correct it, the correction holds 98.7 percent of the time. You notice when it goes wrong.

ChatGPT fails quietly

101 corrections. Smooth to work with. Perfect correction efficiency. And it silently dropped 55 percent of instructions without ever triggering a correction. The low effort is not evidence of alignment. It is evidence that the failure is invisible.

One fails loudly. The other fails quietly. The quiet one is more dangerous, because silent decay is exactly how a person ends up rating a conversation highly while it drifts from what they asked for.

THE DECISIONS BEHIND IT

The choices
that matter.

Choose a decision to see the reason
and the boundary it sets.

01 / 04

Tag both sides of the conversation.

Every tool scores the model's turn. This one also scores the person's turn: what kind of steering they did, how often, and whether it worked. That second column is the whole paper. Without it, a high-maintenance model and a safe one look identical.

02 / 04

Use the conversations I already had.

Petri and Bloom generate conversations with a simulated user. Those are built to be representative of a bad experience, which is useful. Mine were not built at all. They are the actual work: code, compliance analysis, writing, research. The drift in them is the drift that happens.

03 / 04

Never let the auditor see the generator's context.

Found by failing seven simpler ways first. If you debug a conversation with the same model that produced it, the debugger inherits the priors that caused the drift and misses it. The auditor runs separately, on the transcript alone.

04 / 04

One person, both models.

Same operator, same habits, same expectations across all 512 conversations. That is a limit on how far the result travels. It is also the control: any difference in operator load belongs to the model, not to the person.

WHERE IT STANDS

The limits,
stated plainly.

The scoring weights are heuristic, not calibrated against a labeled set of confirmed drift events. The 512-conversation comparison was produced with the tool's first-stage keyword detection; the second-stage model verifier shipped later, so the cross-model table is a working hypothesis, not a settled finding. "Instruction survival" there means the instruction's words kept appearing in the model's answers, a proxy for compliance. One operator controls for variance and limits how far the numbers generalize. All of this is in the paper and in the repository's README.

What the work shows regardless: a question nobody was asking, a tool that asks it, real data to ask it on, and an answer that holds up to the first round of scrutiny and says where the next round should look.
